research

agency, learning, and the organisation of adaptive behaviour

My research addresses the comparative problem of agency: what makes a system an agent, and how do the mechanisms and control processes underlying agential behaviour vary across species and substrates? I approach this question through both theoretical analysis and experimental work, with reinforcement learning serving as a central framework for operationalising agency.

My doctoral dissertation, Reinforcement learning and the organisation of agency: a constraint-theoretic account, develops the idea that constraints and divergent evolutionary histories shape the control architectures that enable adaptive behaviour. Rather than treating agency as a single threshold property, I argue for a graded, organisation-centred account in which learning and decision-making are functionally interdependent.

themes

The comparative scope of reinforcement learning

How far does reinforcement learning extend as a framework for understanding agency? I examine its functional variation across different species and systems, and what its successes and limits reveal about the organisation of adaptive behaviour.

Cognition beyond neural systems

Through the Non-Neural Cognition Lab, I study agency and decision-making in systems without nervous systems — particularly the acellular slime mould Physarum polycephalum — combining experimental work with conceptual analysis of what such organisms can teach us about minimal agency.

Control architectures and the structure of agency

I'm interested in how control structures support adaptive strategies for learning and decision-making, and in heterarchical (rather than strictly hierarchical) models of how agency is organised.

Self-modelling, interoception & the predictive mind

Earlier and ongoing work examines self-modelling under active inference, including oppositional self-modelling in Tourette Syndrome and the interplay between interoceptive and conceptual representation.
